- A4,5,14--**DAAE attack** is a defense traffic transformation learning (defense-aware) mechanism that enhances existing website fingerprinting (WF) attacks. This method aims to learn defense-induced transformations, particularly dummy packet injection mechanisms, from paired original and defended traces using a deep learning model, without requiring access to the defense's internal implementation (black-box). DAAE then identifies and removes likely dummy packets to recover traffic patterns closer to the original traces. The processed traces are subsequently fed into existing WF classifiers, such as RF, NetCLR, and Var-CNN. Unlike adversarial training, DAAE aims to reverse defense-induced traffic obfuscation to facilitate WF attacks. Further details on DAAE's design and evaluation are available in our anonymized artifact. We will provide a more intuitive description of the DAAE attack, including its learning objective, workflow, and integration with existing WF classifiers. We will also clarify whether DAAE is considered in the real-world evaluation and explain the threat model involving multiple independently randomized defended traces. Additionally, we will clarify the meaning of injectivity boundaries, the interpretation of FPR and other evaluation metrics from the defense perspective, and the threshold selection used in ROC-based evaluations. Finally, we will better explain the practical implications of the reported F1 scores, justify the choice of ρ, and clarify the theoretical basis of the uncertainty argument. We will also streamline the presentation to emphasize the key findings and contributions.

- A8,9,10--First, in the open-world evaluation, lower attack FPR can indicate that the attacker is more effective at identifying monitored websites without incorrectly classifying unmonitored websites. Adaptive Tamaraw borrowed the idea of utilizing traffic cluster anonymization from Palette to defend against WF attacks, and introduced a dynamic traffic clustering mechanism for unmonitored traces. Each unmonitored website trace has significantly different traffic patterns. The patterns within each trace may not cluster with the monitored traces using a k-anonymity-based clustering algorithm. This will significantly reduce the FPR for the unmonitored dataset. We agree that our original interpretation of FPR was misleading: a lower FPR benefits the attacker by reducing false alarms, whereas a defense ideally reduces TPR and increases FPR. We will revise the comparison to clarify this distinction. Second, the ROC curves are generated by varying the decision threshold of each WF attack classifier to distinguish monitored from unmonitored traffic. This threshold belongs to the attack classifier and is independent of the configuration of the underlying defense mechanism. The corresponding AUC measures the classifier's discrimination capability across different operating thresholds. Finally, F1-score measures the attacker's overall identification effectiveness by balancing precision and recall; a lower F1 indicates reduced attack capability but does not directly represent an absolute privacy guarantee or the percentage of users deanonymized. We will clarify the practical implications and limitations of these metrics by interpreting F1 together with TPR, FPR, and precision.

- B1--We thank the reviewer for raising this important concern. HiLoD is not intended to approximate the latent space of a particular attacker; rather, it provides an efficient, model-independent heuristic for identifying candidate traces with high intra-class diversity and low inter-class separability. As described in Section IV-A, TraceToFeature captures complementary traffic characteristics, including packet volume, temporal dynamics, packet direction, and burst structure. These features summarize observable properties of packet-level traffic that are also available to deep WF classifiers, although deep models may learn additional nonlinear representations. To justify the effectiveness of measuring similarity in this handcrafted feature space, we conducted an additional experiment on the DF dataset, which contains 95 monitored websites and 1,000 traces per website. We selected the same number of candidate traces (19,000) using HiLoD and random selection, respectively, and evaluated the resulting defended traffic using the same DF attack model. HiLoD achieved an attack accuracy of 19.03%, whereas random selection yielded an average accuracy of 86.33% across four independent runs, with the worst-case accuracy reaching 90.75%. These results demonstrate that selecting candidates based on traffic characteristics is substantially more effective than selecting them randomly. Without accounting for intra-class diversity and inter-class separability, random selection may cause a large proportion of traces to be morphed toward traces belonging to the same class, resulting in less diverse and less effective traffic transformations and, consequently, significantly weakening the defense.

The detailed threat model, system design, architectural specifications, and evaluation results are available in our anonymous artifacts README file. **Threat model and alignment-derived supervision**: DAAE follows a black-box threat model: the adversary knows that traffic is defended but does not know the defense’s internal implementation or actual dummy-packet insertion positions. The adversary obtains paired original and defended traces by collecting the same browsing visits through a comparable PT deployment, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The original traces are captured at the Tor client, while the corresponding defended traces are captured between the PT client and PT server. Thus, the paired data are available to the adversary without requiring access to the defense’s internal state. We use sequence alignment to derive approximate supervision masks from these paired traces. Specifically, LCS identifies a common packet-direction subsequence between the original and defended traces, allowing unmatched positions in the defended trace to be treated as candidate dummy packets. We acknowledge that repeated packet directions can result in multiple valid alignments. Therefore, the resulting masks are alignment-derived estimates rather than ground-truth injection labels. The result shows that the without and with LCS burst features, the attack accuracy of the Var-CNN model can increase from 54.97% to 88.71% under DF dataset, and increase the accuracy from 48.34% to 78.82% for the GTT23 dataset.We have also conducted additional experiments evaluating DAAE against multiple existing defenses, including alignment-derived supervision and component validation in our anonymous artifacts README file.


- C1--In the design of Chameleon, we have considered the situation of reverse engineering or candidate pool leakage. We acknowledge that the website trace candidate pool distributed with each client may be accessible to an attacker and should not be considered secret. Chameleon explicitly accounts for this potential information leakage through a many-to-many morphing strategy. Specifically, each traffic trace from one or more websites can be transformed into multiple distinct morphing traces, while each morphing trace can correspond to multiple website traces or multiple traces from one website. Consequently, even if the attacker obtains the entire profile pool, knowing the candidate profiles alone is insufficient to uniquely infer the original website. A stronger attack would need to overcome this many-to-many mapping ambiguity rather than simply exploit knowledge of the profile pool.

- D2--DAAE requires paired original and defended traces from the same browsing visits to learn the defense-induced packet transformation. In our threat model, an adversary can obtain these pairs by operating a comparable PT deployment that incorporates the target defense. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 2, the adversary captures the original Tor trace at the client and the corresponding defended trace between the PT client and PT server during the same visit. This requires neither access to the defense's internal implementation nor access to the victim's browsing sessions. In our evaluation, we use 20% of the instances from any dataset applied. The remaining 80% are reserved for evaluation and are not paired with original traces. For example, GTT23 contains 100 domains with 2,000 instances per domain, 200,000 traces in total for the closed-world evaluation. The DAAE support set contains 400 pairs per domain, or 40,000 pairs in total. At inference, DAAE receives only defended traces, without access to their corresponding originals or website labels.  To examine whether DAAE generalizes beyond the deployment from which its paired support data are obtained, we evaluate it in two settings: (1) a within-bridge setting (one client to one entry node) using the DF dataset and (2) a cross-bridge setting (multiple clients to multiple entry nodes) using the GTT23 dataset (GTT23 dataset collected at the Exit Node, which includes the website traces from different clients for each website). We evaluate DAAE in both within-bridge and cross-bridge settings, using DF and GTT23, respectively. Under the closed-world Var-CNN evaluation on DF, DAAE increases attack accuracy from 2.49% to 88.71%, substantially outperforming the adversarial-training-based attack, which reaches only 45.02% from the same 2.49% baseline. More importantly, under the cross-bridge setting on GTT23, DAAE still increases attack accuracy from 2.11% to 78.82%. These results demonstrate that DAAE can recover substantial attack effectiveness from defended traffic, even when evaluated across bridge settings. We address these concerns through additional experiments and clarifications in the DAAE artifact README, including closed-world attack accuracy, open-world TPR and FPR with seven SOTA defense models.
